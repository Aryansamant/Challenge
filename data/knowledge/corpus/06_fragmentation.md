Haddad et al. (2015) synthesized fragmentation experiments and observational studies. Fragmentation reduces biodiversity by 13–75% depending on the fragment and taxon, and impairs nutrient cycling and biomass. Losses accumulate for at least a decade; they are not a one-time species count drop.

Newbold et al. (2015, Nature) showed land-use change has already reduced local species richness and abundance globally, with the strongest effects in converted and intensively used lands. IPBES (2019) ranks land-use change as the top historical driver of terrestrial biodiversity loss.

Connectivity thresholds: when remaining habitat falls toward ~10–20% of a landscape, dispersal fails for many area-sensitive species (see also Andrén 1994). Hedgerows, riparian strips and agroforestry trees function as corridors or stepping stones. On a single farm, converting the worst 5–10% of land (wet corners, steep edges) to native linear habitat is often the least-cost way to move the landscape back above a connectivity threshold.

Deforestation also dries and heats remaining fragments (edge effects), accelerating SOC loss — a land–climate–soil cascade, not a trees-only issue.
