Jose (2009) reviews agroforestry as a multifunctional land-use that jointly produces food, carbon storage, biodiversity habitat and erosion control. Trees alter microclimate (lower wind speed, lower daytime soil temperature, sometimes hydraulic lift), add perennial root carbon, and create vertical structure used by birds, bats and arthropods that are absent from short-stature monocultures.

Carbon: litter and root turnover typically raise SOC most under the tree crown and in the crop–tree interface. Biodiversity: even low densities of scattered trees (parklands) increase bird and insect richness relative to treeless fields. Water: competition is the central agronomic risk; deciduous nitrogen-fixing trees with deep roots (e.g. Faidherbia albida, which reverse-phenology in parts of Africa) are classic designs to reduce peak competition.

Sileshi and others later showed fertilizer-tree systems (Gliricidia, Faidherbia) can raise maize yields on depleted African soils while adding woody habitat — a soil–yield–biodiversity coupling rather than a conservation set-aside.
