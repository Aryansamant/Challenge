FAO (2017) frames soil organic carbon (SOC) as the hidden potential of land: it is simultaneously a climate pool, a water-holding sponge, and the energy base of the soil food web. Typical cultivated soils contain on the order of 0.5–4% organic carbon in the topsoil. Values near 0.3% in semi-arid cropland indicate a biologically exhausted mineral matrix with weak aggregation.

Low SOC reduces available water capacity (Hudson 1994 showed a near-linear relationship between organic matter and plant-available water). It also starves heterotrophic microbes and soil fauna, collapsing nutrient mineralization and mycorrhizal networks that native plants depend on. Erosion then exports the remaining C-rich topsoil, a positive feedback documented by Lal (2003, 2004).

Management that raises SOC — cover crops, organic amendments, agroforestry, residue retention — therefore moves three variables at once: carbon, water, and habitat for soil biodiversity. The “4 per 1000” initiative (Minasny et al. 2017, Geoderma) argues that even ~0.4% relative annual increases in global SOC stocks would be climatically material; on depleted fields the relative biological gains are larger than the climate accounting suggests.

Practical implication: if SOC is critically low and rainfall is unreliable, water-harvesting plus living-root cover is a higher-leverage first move than fertilizer alone, because nutrients leak from unstructured soil.
